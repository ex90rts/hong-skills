# Stage Checklists

Use the relevant section to assess evidence and select the next coaching move. Do not recite every question to the learner. Choose the smallest subset that exposes the most important uncertainty.

## Prepare

### Inspect

- Is the learning object narrow enough for the available time?
- What real situation, problem, decision, or creation will use it?
- Is the target awareness, explanation, transfer, or independent operation?
- What observable result will count as success?
- Does the acceptance task match the intended mastery level?
- Are the sources credible, current enough, appropriately difficult, and relevant?
- Is the source set small enough to begin rather than keep collecting?
- Is the sequence broken into executable units with outputs?

### Evidence

- A learning contract with subject, application, mastery target, and acceptance evidence.
- A bounded source set with a reason for each source.
- A sequence whose next unit can be started immediately.

### Common failure signals

- The goal is broad, prestigious, or abstract but has no application.
- The learner keeps collecting sources without a stop rule.
- The plan allocates time but defines no stage outputs.
- The acceptance task measures recognition while the target requires execution.

## Input

### Inspect

- Can the learner identify the core concepts and key facts?
- Can they describe causal or logical relations and the conditions under which they hold?
- Can they supply an example, a counterexample, or an important boundary?
- Can they connect the material to prior knowledge without forcing a false analogy?
- Can they state what remains unexplained?
- Can they explain a conclusion in their own words?
- Can they predict what changes when a condition changes?
- Can they distinguish nearby concepts and their use conditions?
- Did the input create new, useful questions?

### Evidence

- A compact concept-and-relationship map.
- Explanations or predictions not copied from the source.
- A question backlog that separates blocking from non-blocking gaps.

### Common failure signals

- Fast consumption, highlighting, or summaries are offered as proof of learning.
- The learner repeats terminology but cannot explain mechanisms.
- One difficult detail consumes the session despite not blocking the goal.
- The learner accepts source claims without checking assumptions or alternatives.

## Reflect

### Inspect

- What root problem does the knowledge solve?
- What model or principle did the learner use before studying?
- What model do they use now, and what changed?
- Which prior assumptions were wrong or untested?
- How does the new model connect to existing knowledge?
- What mechanisms, principles, and necessary conditions can be extracted?
- What boundaries, counterexamples, and uncertainties remain?
- Can the learner reorganize the ideas instead of following the source sequence?
- Can they reconstruct the core structure with the source closed?

### Evidence

- A closed-source reconstruction in the learner's own organization.
- A before-and-after model comparison.
- Explicit mechanisms, conditions, boundaries, and uncertainties.

### Common failure signals

- The learner performs a shallow recap or paraphrase.
- Reflection starts before basic input gaps are repaired.
- The learner looks repeatedly at the source while claiming reconstruction.
- The learner jumps directly to a new scenario without first extracting a model.

## Transfer

### Inspect

- What cognitive structure, mechanism, or model is being transferred?
- Which features and conditions define a matching situation?
- Does the new situation actually satisfy them?
- Which surface differences are irrelevant, and which break the analogy?
- What side effects or risks could application create?
- How will success or failure be detected?
- Can the learner find a less obvious medium- or far-transfer case?
- Is the learner applying a principle or recalling what the original example did?

### Evidence

- At least one plausible new scenario with an explicit structure match.
- A list of required conditions, mismatches, risks, and a test.
- A boundary case where the model should not be used.

### Common failure signals

- Only near-identical cases can be found.
- The scenario is fictional and disconnected from any familiar or future activity.
- The learner assumes an analogy from surface resemblance.
- The transfer hypothesis is described as if it were already proven in practice.

## Output And Practice

### Inspect

- Is the output reorganized in the learner's own structure?
- Does it use original examples and explain important causal relations?
- Can the learner answer one level of follow-up?
- Can they perform without copying a demonstration step by step?
- Can they detect abnormal signals and adjust instead of freezing?
- Does an explanation lead to action, and can an action be explained?
- Does the task reproduce the constraints that matter in real use?

### Evidence

- An explanation, lesson, written synthesis, test, artifact, simulation, or real result.
- A comparison against criteria defined during preparation.
- A record of decisions, errors, corrections, and unresolved gaps.

### Common failure signals

- The learner copies the source structure or wording.
- Output is postponed until an undefined feeling of readiness.
- Practice follows a worked example too closely to show independence.
- A large deviation appears but its cause is not investigated.

## Feedback

### Inspect

- Did the answer, artifact, program, performance, or business result meet the target?
- What did a teacher, peer, expert, user, or reviewer specifically observe?
- Where did recall stop, explanation become vague, prediction fail, or action diverge?
- Does the feedback say where, why, and by how much performance differed?
- Is the signal about goals, information, the mental model, context recognition, expression, or execution?
- What is the earliest stage that can correct the detected cause?
- What small experiment can distinguish competing diagnoses?

### Evidence

- A concrete expected-versus-observed comparison.
- A diagnosis tied to evidence and given a confidence level.
- A return stage and bounded corrective action.

### Common failure signals

- Vague praise or criticism is treated as diagnostic evidence.
- Too many low-quality signals drown out important feedback.
- Only the final outcome is inspected; the learner's decisions remain invisible.
- The learner repeats practice even though the goal or model is wrong.

## Retrieval Sidecar

### Inspect

- Which concepts, decisions, procedures, boundaries, or former errors have high future value?
- Can the learner recall or reconstruct them without cues?
- Can they explain, predict, or apply them in a fresh context?
- Did recall fail because of memory, weak understanding, a wrong model, or missing practice?
- Should the next retrieval interval shorten, lengthen, or stay unchanged?

### Evidence

- A small retrieval queue with prompts rather than copied answers.
- The next review point and the reason for its timing.
- A history of recall quality and corrective routing.

### Common failure signals

- Review means rereading everything.
- Notes preserve conclusions but contain no retrieval prompts.
- A recurring conceptual distortion is treated only as forgetting.
- Review volume grows without prioritizing value or weakness.
